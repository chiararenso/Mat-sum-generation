\begin{abstract}
Sharing human-mobility data is a widely debated issue because of the
tension between the utility of such data for urban analysis and the
need to preserve privacy and confidentiality. Public mobility datasets
are relatively few, often sparse, and in some cases outdated, whereas
richer and more recent data are typically held by service providers
that may be reluctant to release them because of privacy, confidentiality,
and data-ownership concerns. This has motivated growing interest in
synthetic mobility generation.

In this work, we address this problem from a semantic perspective.
Rather than generating mobility directly in geographic space, we study
whether a semantic abstraction can provide an effective representation
for synthetic mobility generation. We also focus on the small-data
setting, asking whether a limited confidential mobility dataset can be
sufficient to generate a useful, privacy-aware synthetic surrogate.

Our approach uses a state-of-the-art semantic trajectory summarization
method to construct the semantic representation on which generation
takes place. We evaluate the role of the representation, the effect of
training-set size, and the privacy/fidelity trade-off associated with
generator capacity on two mobility datasets.

Our results show that semantic abstraction produces a more learnable
generative state space than matched geometric representations, remains
effective in small-data regimes, and interacts with generator capacity
through a clear privacy/fidelity trade-off. Overall, semantic
abstraction directly shapes both the learnability and the empirical
disclosure properties of synthetic mobility generation. 

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Human-mobility data support a wide range of applications in
transportation, urban planning, epidemiology, and behavioral analysis.
However, such data are difficult to share. Mobility records can be
highly identifying even after explicit identifiers have been removed,
while service providers may also be reluctant to release valuable
proprietary datasets because of confidentiality and data-ownership
concerns.

Synthetic mobility data offer a possible alternative: rather than
releasing the original records, a data owner can learn from a
confidential population and release newly generated trajectories that
preserve relevant population-level properties. The challenge is to
retain sufficient analytical utility without reproducing patterns that
are specific to individual training records.

Most synthetic mobility approaches address this problem primarily in
geometric space, representing trajectories through coordinates, grid
cells, spatial clusters, or mobility graphs~\cite{feng2020,gursoy2018}.
This is natural when geometric fidelity is the main objective.
However, many analyses are interested in the \emph{meaning} of visited
places---for example, residential areas, transport facilities,
commercial locations, or green spaces. In this setting, the
representation is not merely an encoding choice: it defines the state
space over which the generative model must learn mobility dynamics.

This observation motivates the central question of this work:
\emph{can semantic abstraction itself provide the state space in which
synthetic mobility is generated?}
We build on MAT-Sum~\cite{pugliese2023}, a semantic trajectory
summarization method that groups geographic areas according to their
semantic context. We repurpose the resulting semantic locations as the
states of the generative process. Each trajectory becomes an ordered
sequence of visits to a finite semantic vocabulary, and generation is
performed directly in this abstract space.

Our target scenario is a data owner wishing to release a synthetic
surrogate of a confidential mobility population while limiting the
amount of raw data exposed to the synthesis process. This may reflect a
data-minimization principle or, for example, the need to delegate
generation without transferring the complete confidential collection.
A smaller training sample is not assumed to be inherently more private:
while it reduces raw-data exposure, too few individuals may make each
training record more influential. We therefore explicitly study how
training-set size affects both fidelity and empirical disclosure.

The resulting framework is deliberately \emph{representation-first}.
We study three design factors---representation, amount of confidential
training data, and generator capacity---along two complementary
dimensions. \emph{Semantic fidelity} measures whether the generated data
preserve the mobility structure of interest, whereas
\emph{empirical disclosure} measures exact reproduction or unusual
proximity to individual training records. We do not claim a formal
differential-privacy guarantee.

This leads to the following research questions:

\begin{description}

\item[\textbf{RQ1: Role of the abstraction.}]
When generating semantically rich mobility data, what is gained by
constructing the generative state space from semantics rather than
geometry?

\item[\textbf{RQ2: Data scale and empirical disclosure.}]
How do semantic fidelity and empirical record-level disclosure risk
change as the number of individuals available in the confidential
training set increases?

\item[\textbf{RQ3: Sequence generator and privacy--fidelity trade-off.}]
Under the same MAT-Sum representation, how do an order-1 Markov model
and a higher-capacity LSTM compare in semantic fidelity and empirical
record-level disclosure risk across data regimes?

\item[\textbf{RQ4: Disclosure resistance and utility of the release.}]
Beyond the record-level measures used to compare design choices in
RQ1--RQ3, is the final released configuration resistant to realistic
disclosure attacks -- including a shadow-model membership-inference
attack -- and does it retain downstream utility?

\end{description}

We answer these questions through controlled experiments on two
substantially different mobility datasets, Paris-OSM and GeoLife.
RQ1 varies the representation while controlling state-space size and generator; RQ2 varies the number of training individuals; RQ3 compares Markov and LSTM generators under the same MAT-Sum representation; and RQ4 audits the resulting released configuration directly, rather than comparing design alternatives.

The results provide four main findings. First, semantic abstraction not only preserves semantic information better than matched geometric representations, as expected, but also produces a transition space that the same generator can reproduce more accurately. Second, useful semantic generation remains possible with small confidential samples,
although the benefit of additional users depends on how much mobility information each individual contributes. Third, generator capacitymcreates a privacy/fidelity trade-off: the LSTM better captures higher-order sequential structure, while the order-1 Markov provides better first-order fidelity and a more conservative empirical disclosure profile when training data are scarce. Fourth, a dedicated disclosure audit of the released configuration -- copy and proximity checks, a shadow-model membership-inference attack, and a unicity analysis -- finds no reliable membership signal and markedly lower re-identifiability than the real data, while the release retains first-order downstream utility.

Taken together, these results support a representation-first view of privacy-aware synthetic mobility generation. The main contribution is not a new sequence generator, but the demonstration that semantic
abstraction can define an effective generative state space for small confidential mobility datasets: semantics not only describe the information to preserve, but also structure the space in which  generation takes place.


\section{Background and Related Work}

The current work lies at the intersection of semantic trajectory
representation, synthetic mobility generation, and privacy-aware
mobility data release.

\textbf{Semantic trajectories representation}
Semantic trajectories enrich raw movement with semantic information \cite{spaccapietra2008} and their latest definition includes the enrichment of heterogeneous
semantic aspects, bringing to the definition of the so-called Multiple Aspects Trajectories~\cite{bogorny2019}. These trajectories tend to be large not only in the number of GPS points but also in the number of heterogeneous semantic features, which can be complex to represent and redundant when linked to each spatio-temporal point. 
The approach proposed in MAT-Sum~\cite{pugliese2023} mitigates this limitation by adopting a \emph{location-centric} view. Here, the enrichment happens at the geographical location level, and the trajectories are enriched with the semantics of the underlying crossed area. Practically, MAT-Sum tessellates an area, enriches tiles with the different semantic aspects (e.g. POIs, land use, public transport), merges the tiles with identical or similar semantic context into uniform semantic locations. Then it
summarizes each trajectory as a weighted set over them. The summarization capacity is quantified by a summarization rate
($S_{\text{rate}}$) and a semantic quality preservation (MUITAS~\cite{petry2019}). In this work, we retain the temporal order of visits to these semantic
locations and use the resulting ordered semantic sequence as the
representation on which generation takes place.
We reuse MAT-Sum's location-centric semantic abstraction and
repurpose the resulting semantic locations as the state space for
synthetic mobility generation.

\textbf{Synthetic Mobility Data }
It is useful to distinguish two families of synthetic mobility data. Simulation-based (bottom-up) methods generate movement from behavioral rules like the recent agent-based patterns-of-life microsimulations~\cite{zuefle_pol}, or the most well known traffic and activity-based simulators such as SUMO~\cite{krajzewicz2002}. 
In this line of approaches the output reflects generic plausible behavior, and it is not a faithful surrogate of any specific real population.

The Data-driven (top-down) methods instead learn a model from a real dataset and sample new trajectories that reproduce and expand its structure.
In this last field of mobility data generation, we have approaches based on mechanistic models . These approaches reproduce empirical mobility regularities
such as visitation frequency, radius of gyration, jump length, and waiting time through mechanisms such as exploration and preferential
return~\cite{song2010,pappalardo2018}. Their main advantage is
interpretability and the explicit modeling of mobility mechanisms. However, privacy protection is generally not their primary design objective and, when models are calibrated on confidential data, disclosure must be assessed separately.

Another line of data-driven approaches uses deep generative
models~\cite{feng2020,rao2020,zhu2023}. These models can capture
complex spatio-temporal dependencies that are difficult to represent with simpler mobility models. However, because they are learned from individual trajectories, high generative fidelity alone does not imply privacy: dedicated privacy mechanisms or empirical disclosure
evaluation are needed.

More recently, diffusion models have emerged as a powerful alternative for data-driven synthetic mobility generation. DiffTraj~\cite{zhu2023difftraj}
models GPS trajectories through a spatio-temporal diffusion process, reconstructing geographic trajectories from noise with a trajectory-specific denoising network. The same line of work led to SynMob~\cite{zhu2023synmob}, a publicly released synthetic GPS trajectory dataset designed to retain
the geo-distribution and statistical properties of real mobility data.
Subsequent approaches further incorporate mobility-specific structure: ST-DiffTraj~\cite{wang2024stdifftraj} explicitly models temporal and spatio-temporal dependencies during denoising. 
These approaches demonstrate the effectiveness of
high-capacity diffusion models for reproducing complex mobility
distributions. However, they mainly operate on geographic or spatio-temporal trajectory representations. 

Recent work has begun to incorporate higher-level or semantic-aware representations into trajectory generation. However, the use of a semantic trajectory summarization as the data-dependent generative state space for producing a surrogate of a specific confidential population remains comparatively unexplored. In particular, the interaction between semantic representation, limited confidential training data, generator capacity, and empirical record-level disclosure has received little attention. This is the gap we target.

Our work is based on the assumption that the objective of a data owner is to release a shareable surrogate of a specific confidential dataset. Under this hypothesis, we need to target the data-driven family. 
But this is exactly why privacy is a live concern here: the generator ingests real individual trajectories, it can memorize and leak them. %Leakage must be measured rather than assumed away. 
Our contribution is a representation-first framework for
privacy-aware, data-driven semantic mobility synthesis, in which
MAT-Sum defines the state space on which generation takes place.
We additionally treat the amount of confidential training data as a
design variable, asking whether a useful surrogate can be learned
from a deliberately limited sample and how this choice affects
empirical disclosure.

A recent work~\cite{kavalionak2026} addresses data-driven mobility generation from a complementary perspective. Their approach abstracts movement by spatially clustering GPS points into regions, constructs a weighted mobility graph, and generates trajectories through a second-order Markov random walk subsequently projected onto the road network.

Two main differences characterize our contribution. First, their
abstraction is primarily spatial: the generative states correspond to geometric regions, whereas MAT-Sum constructs the state space from contextual semantics. Second, the primary evaluation emphasis differs.
Their work focuses on population-level spatial fidelity and
graph-structural properties, whereas our controlled experiments focus on semantic fidelity and empirical individual-level disclosure.
Specifically, semantic fidelity is evaluated through $n$-gram
distributional distances, while disclosure is assessed through exact and near copies, MUITAS-based distance to the closest training record, membership inference, and unicity.

Our release-level analysis additionally considers aggregate geographic
utility, but without reconstructing individual GPS trajectories.
The two approaches are therefore complementary: one emphasizes
geometry-centered generation and aggregate spatial structure, whereas
ours studies semantic representation as the generative substrate and
its interaction with fidelity, limited confidential data, and
individual-level disclosure.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}


Our central design choice is to separate the representation used
for generation from the sequence model that operates on it.
Rather than learning directly from raw coordinates or geometric cells,
we first use MAT-Sum~\cite{pugliese2023} to construct a finite set of semantic locations. In the original MAT-Sum formulation, these locations are used to summarize each trajectory as a weighted set.
For generation, we adapt this representation by retaining the temporal order in which a trajectory visits the MAT-Sum semantic locations.
The resulting trajectory is therefore represented as an ordered,
temporally weighted sequence of visits to a finite semantic vocabulary. Our sequence generators operate on these ordered semantic sequences rather than on the weighted-set representation used for summarization or on the original GPS points. We then learn a sequence generator over this state space.

This separation allows us to study the three factors 
addressed by our research questions independently: the effect of the abstraction (RQ1), the amount of private data available to estimate the model (RQ2), and the capacity of the sequence generator (RQ3).

Figure~\ref{fig:methodology} summarizes the pipeline.
The central design choice is to separate the semantic representation from the sequence generator: MAT-Sum defines the state space, while the Markov or LSTM models operate exclusively on the resulting semantic sequences.

\subsection{Semantic abstraction}
\label{sec:semantic-abstraction}

We use MAT-Sum~\cite{pugliese2023} to construct the semantic locations that define our generative vocabulary. MAT-Sum tessellates the geographic area, enriches the resulting cells with semantic aspects, and groups cells sharing a semantic context into semantic locations.

The semantic aspects are drawn from a fixed, public OpenStreetMap taxonomy of about $65$ labels spanning
four families: \emph{points of interest} (e.g.\ accommodation, food and beverages, retail, healthcare,
education, office, leisure, cultural and historic attractions), \emph{land use} (residential, natural,
water, farmland, industrial), \emph{public transport} (bus, train, tram, subway, rail stops and stations),
and the \emph{road network} (pedestrian, cycle and motor roads). Each tile is described by the aspects
present in it, ranked by TF--IDF; a semantic location retains its top-$m$ aspects as a compact
multi-aspect \emph{signature}. For $m{=}2$, typical signatures are single- or two-aspect sets such as
$\{\textsf{residential}\}$, $\{\textsf{accommodation}, \textsf{food\_and\_beverages}\}$,
$\{\textsf{natural}, \textsf{residential}\}$ or $\{\textsf{bus}, \textsf{transportation}\}$. A trajectory
is thus a sequence of such signatures with dwell times, e.g.\
$\{\textsf{residential}\}\!\rightarrow\!\{\textsf{bus},\textsf{transportation}\}\!\rightarrow\!
\{\textsf{accommodation},\textsf{food\_and\_beverages}\}\!\rightarrow\!\{\textsf{residential}\}$.


We then map each raw trajectory to the temporally ordered sequence of semantic locations it traverses. Consecutive observations associated with the same semantic location are run-length collapsed into a single visit, while their elapsed time is accumulated as dwell time.
The resulting representation is
\[
S_{u,r} =
\left[
(z_1,\delta_1),\ldots,(z_L,\delta_L)
\right],
\]
where $z_i$ is a MAT-Sum semantic location and $\delta_i$ is the
corresponding dwell time.

\subsection{Minimum-support semantic vocabulary}
\label{sec:vocabulary}

As we are in the scenario where we need to create data driven synthetic data from possibly small  and confidential datasets.  We have to consider that a vocabulary built from a small sample may contain rare semantic contexts that are weakly supported and potentially identifying the individual.

The parameter $m$ controls the semantic resolution of the representation by retaining the $m$ most informative semantic aspects associated with each location. Smaller values produce coarser semantic states, whereas larger values distinguish more specific contexts and typically induce a larger generative vocabulary.
The parameter $k$, instead, controls the minimum support required for a semantic state to remain in the vocabulary; states with insufficient support are generalized toward similar, better-supported states.
We therefore apply a minimum-support generalization controlled by a parameter $k$.
Similarity between two semantic locations is measured by
their Jaccard similarity.


The resulting vocabulary is denoted by
%
\[
    \mathcal{V}_{m,k}(\mathcal{D}).
\]
%
A key property of the experiments in RQ2 and RQ3 is that the vocabulary is reconstructed from the individuals present in each training sample.
Consequently, its effective cardinality may grow with the amount of training data. The public semantic map remains fixed and only the data-dependent vocabulary and the generative model are re-estimated.


\subsection{Sequence generation}
\label{sec:generation}

Given the semantic sequences induced by
$\mathcal{V}_{m,k}(\mathcal{D})$, we model separately
(i) sequence length, (ii) semantic-state transitions, and (iii) dwell times. This decoupling ensures that the comparison between different sequence generators changes the transition model while keeping the remaining components fixed.

Our main generator is a first-order Markov chain over the semantic vocabulary. Let
%
\[
    C_{ij}
\]
%
be the number of observed transitions from state $i$ to state $j$ in the training sequences. With Laplace smoothing, the transition probability is
%
\[
    P(z_{t+1}=j \mid z_t=i)
    =
    \frac{C_{ij}+1}
         {\sum_{j'} C_{ij'} + |\mathcal{V}_{m,k}|}.
\]

The sequence length is sampled independently from the empirical distribution of training-sequence lengths. Dwell times are sampled from the empirical per-state dwell-time distribution. Thus, once a semantic sequence
%
\[
    \widetilde{S}
    =
    [\tilde z_1,\ldots,\tilde z_L]
\]
%
has been generated, each visit receives a newly sampled dwell time associated with its semantic state.

No transition is introduced across independent recording sessions. When a user contributes multiple sessions, transition counts are accumulated within sessions, and the sequence model is reset at each session boundary.

To study the effect of sequence-model capacity in RQ3, we use a lightweight LSTM (Long Short Term Memory) as a higher-capacity alternative. The LSTM operates on
the same data-dependent semantic vocabulary and estimates
%
\[
    P_{\theta}
    (z_t \mid z_1,\ldots,z_{t-1}).
\]

The purpose of this comparison is to change the conditional model used to generate the semantic state sequence. Therefore, for each paired experimental run, Markov and LSTM receive the same training users, the same MAT-Sum vocabulary, and the same empirical length and dwell-time models.

The architecture and training hyperparameters used in each dataset are reported in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\subsection{Controlled geometric abstractions}
\label{sec:baselines}

As stated in our RQ1, we want to isolate the effect of the representation. We therefore compare
MAT-Sum with two purely geometric abstractions while holding the sequence generator and state-space budget fixed: the grid cells and the spatial clustering. 

In the grid representation, the study area is discretized into spatial grid cells, and each visited cell becomes a sequence state.
In the spatial clustering approach, the GPS observations are grouped through spatial $K$-means clustering, and the resulting clusters define the sequence states.

For every paired experimental sample, let
%
\[
    K_r
    =
    |\mathcal{V}_{m,k}(\mathcal{D}^{(r)})|
\]
%
be the effective number of MAT-Sum states. The geometric abstractions are configured to produce the same state-space budget $K_r$.
All three representations are then coupled with the same order-1 Markov generator and the same sampling protocol.

This controlled design makes the semantic abstraction the important factor in RQ1: differences cannot be attributed to a larger state space or a more expressive sequence generator.

%\subsection{A common semantic evaluation space}
%\label{sec:common-space}

The three abstractions used to answer RQ1 have different native-state semantics. Moreover, in RQ2 and RQ3, the MAT-Sum vocabulary is rebuilt for each training sample and therefore changes with the number of available users. Direct comparison only in the native state spaces would consequently mix generation error with differences in the representation itself.

To evaluate the pipeline, we need to distinguish where information can be lost. First, the abstraction itself may discard semantic information. Second, the generator may fail to reproduce the dynamics of the abstracted trajectories. Finally, the combination of these two effects determines the semantic fidelity of the generated data.

We therefore evaluate the process at three levels: native-state generation fidelity, abstraction distortion, and final semantic generation fidelity.

We therefore evaluate semantic fidelity in a common public OSM-label space $\mathcal{L}$. For each abstraction $A$, let
%
\[
    \pi_A :
    \mathcal{V}_A \rightarrow \mathcal{L}
\]
%
be the post-hoc mapping from an abstraction state to its corresponding OSM semantic label. The same mapping is applied to both real abstracted sequences and generated sequences. This allows every method to be compared against the same reference OSM-label sequences.

For a collection of sequences $D$, let
%
\[
    P_D^{(q)}
\]
%
denote the empirical distribution of contiguous $q$-grams. We measure the discrepancy between two distributions $P$ and $Q$ using total variation (TV) distance,
%
\[
    \operatorname{TV}(P,Q)
    =
    \frac{1}{2}
    \sum_x |P(x)-Q(x)|,
\]
%
where lower values indicate greater distributional fidelity.
Total Variation distance is particularly well-suited to our setting because it quantifies how faithfully the synthetic data reproduces the empirical semantic transition distribution in a common, fixed label space.

\subsubsection{Native-state generation fidelity}

For RQ1, we first measure how accurately the generator reproduces the transition distribution within each abstraction's own state space:
%
\[
    D_{\mathrm{native}}(A)
    =
    \operatorname{TV}
    \left(
        P_{\mathcal{D}_A}^{(2)},
        P_{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_A}^{(2)}
    \right),
\]
%
where $\mathcal{D}_A$ and $\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_A$ are respectively the real and synthetic sequences represented under abstraction $A$.
Since the alphabet differs across abstractions, this metric is
interpreted only as a within-representation generation error.

To quantify how much semantic information is lost before generation, we compare the real trajectories after abstraction and projection back to OSM labels with the original OSM-label reference:
%
\[
    D_{\mathrm{abs}}(A)
    =
    \operatorname{TV}
    \left(
        P_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{OSM}}}^{(2)},
        P_{\pi_A(\mathcal{D}_A)}^{(2)}
    \right).
\]

This quantity isolates the distortion introduced by the abstraction itself, independently of the generator.

The final semantic-generation error is
%
\[
    D_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(q)}(A)
    =
    \operatorname{TV}
    \left(
        P_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{OSM}}}^{(q)},
        P_{\pi_A(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_A)}^{(q)}
    \right).
\]

For RQ1 and RQ2, the primary fidelity measure is the semantic bigram distance $D_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(2)}$. In RQ2, this fixed OSM-label space is particularly important because the native MAT-Sum vocabulary changes with sample size, and native-state TV would therefore be measured against a moving alphabet.

For RQ3 we report both
%
\[
    D_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(2)}
    \qquad\text{and}\qquad
    D_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(3)}.
\]
%
The bigram metric measures first-order transition fidelity, whereas the trigram metric exposes higher-order sequential structure that an order-1 Markov model cannot represent explicitly. Reporting both avoids evaluating the Markov model only on the statistic that it directly estimates.

\subsection{Empirical disclosure measures}
\label{sec:privacy-metrics}

Our privacy analysis is empirical rather than formal: we do not provide a differential-privacy guarantee. For RQ1 to RQ3, we focus on record-level memorization, proximity to the training data via exact and near copies, and distance to the closest record. 
Exact copies capture literal memorization; near copies capture almost reproduced trajectories; DCR asks whether a synthetic record is unusually close to any training individual.

Section~\ref{sec:privacy-utility} complements these measures with a release-level utility analysis, a black-box membership-inference attack, and a unicity study.

\subsubsection{Exact copies}

A generated sequence is an exact copy if its complete semantic state sequence is identical to a sequence in the corresponding training set. For $N$ generated sequences,
%
\[
    C_{\mathrm{exact}}
    =
    \sum_{s\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}
    \mathbb{I}
    \left[
        \exists x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}:
        s=x
    \right].
\]
%
We report this quantity as the number of exact copies among $N=200$ generated sequences.

Since the sequence unit differs across datasets (an aggregated trajectory or a recording session), exact-copy counts are interpreted within each dataset rather than directly compared across datasets.

Exact identity may miss synthetic sequences that reproduce almost all of a training record. We therefore use MUITAS~\cite{petry2019} as a
semantic trajectory-similarity measure. For a generated sequence $s$, we define
%
\[
    q(s)
    =
    \max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}}
    \operatorname{MUITAS}(s,x).
\]
%
A sequence is considered a \textit{near-copy} when
%
\[
    q(s) \geq 0.95.
\]
%
The near-copy rate is
%
\[
    C_{\mathrm{near}}
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{s\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}
    \mathbb{I}[q(s)\geq0.95].
\]

For each synthetic sequence $s$, we define its distance to a training individual $u$ as
%
\[
    d(s,u)
    =
    1 -
    \max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_u}
    \operatorname{MUITAS}(s,x).
\]
%
The synthetic-to-training Distance to the Closest Record (DCR) is then
%
\[
    \operatorname{DCR}_{\mathrm{synth}}
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{s\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}
    \min_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{train}}}
    d(s,u).
\]

To make this quantity interpretable, we compare it with a matched
real-to-real baseline. For a real sequence $x$ belonging to user $u$, we exclude all other sequences of that same user and compute
%
\[
    d_{\mathrm{real}}(x)
    =
    1-
    \max_{\substack{
        v\neq u\\
        y\in\mathcal{D}_v
    }}
    \operatorname{MUITAS}(x,y).
\]
%
When individuals contribute multiple sessions, distances are first averaged within each user and then across users, preventing heavily observed individuals from dominating the baseline.

Finally, we report
%
\[
    \Delta\operatorname{DCR}
    =
    \operatorname{DCR}_{\mathrm{synth}}
    -
    \operatorname{DCR}_{\mathrm{base}}.
\]
%
A positive value means that, according to the chosen semantic similarity measure, synthetic records are on average farther from their closest training individual than real records are from other real individuals. A negative value indicates comparatively greater proximity to the training data.

\subsection{Experimental design}
\label{sec:experimental-design}

RQ1--RQ3 are evaluated through controlled paired experiments that compare design alternatives (representation, training-set size, generator) under matched conditions. RQ4 instead audits the single, final released configuration, and is evaluated separately in Section~\ref{sec:privacy-utility} using the release-level protocol described there.

\paragraph{RQ1: role of the abstraction.}
In this experiment we vary the representation while controlling the generator and state-space budget. Grid, spatial clustering, and MAT-Sum are evaluated on the same user samples, with matched numbers of states and the same order-1 Markov generator. We report native-state bigram TV, common-label abstraction distortion, semantic generation TV, and copy-based disclosure measures.

\paragraph{RQ2: small-data scaling.}
We fix MAT-Sum and the order-1 Markov generator and vary the number of training individuals. Within each replicate, user samples are nested,
%
\[
    \mathcal{D}_{n_1}^{(r)}
    \subset
    \mathcal{D}_{n_2}^{(r)}
    \subset \cdots,
\]
%
so that changes along the scaling curve reflect the addition of individuals rather than unrelated sample composition. The MAT-Sum vocabulary is rebuilt at every $n$. For this reason, quality is evaluated in the fixed OSM-label space rather than by comparing native state distributions across different $n$.

\paragraph{RQ3: sequence model and privacy-fidelity trade-off.}
We fix the MAT-Sum representation and compare the order-1 Markov and LSTM generators on the same paired user samples. Both models share the same vocabulary, empirical length model, dwell-time model, and output size. We measure first-order semantic fidelity through bigram TV, higher-order fidelity through trigram TV, and disclosure through near-copy rate, exact copies, and $\Delta\operatorname{DCR}$.

Unless stated otherwise, each configuration generates $N=200$ synthetic sequences and is evaluated over 20 paired replicates.
We report means and 95\% confidence intervals. For direct paired comparisons, significance is assessed on replicate-wise differences using paired sign-flip permutation tests. Bootstrap confidence intervals of paired effects are reported where appropriate. Dataset-specific sample sizes and preprocessing choices are given in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\subsection{Datasets and protocol}

We test the method on two complementary publicly avaiable mobility datasets: Paris-OSM \cite{pugliese2025}  and Geolife \cite{geolife}.

\textbf{Paris-OSM dataset}
The Paris dataset is obtained from the semantically enriched OSM mobility dataset of~\cite{pugliese2025}: raw GPS traces are merged into one trajectory per de-identified OpenStreetMap contributor, i.e. we have 581
individuals, 1.57\,M points. The merging is per-user, so we will refer to per-trajectory metrics as per-person metrics. The user $ids$ are de-identified.
We ran MAT-Sum map-enrichment on this area (squares, zoom~17): 32{,}697 enriched tiles
$\rightarrow$ 18{,}554 semantic locations.

Paris-OSM is the main dataset where we run our evaluation.
To isolate the contribution of the abstraction itself, we reduce three abstractions of the same data to the same number of states ($\sim$328) and feed each to an identical order-1 Markov generator, so any difference is attributable to how the state space is built, not to the generator. \textsc{Grid-Markov} uses spatial grid cells, \textsc{Cluster-Markov} uses KMeans spatial clusters, and \textsc{MAT-Sum-Markov} uses the $m{=}2,k{=}5$ semantic vocabulary (328 symbols). \textsc{Cluster-Markov} is thus a first-order variant of the weighted graph walk of~\cite{kavalionak2026}; RQ1 isolates the effect of replacing that geometric state space with a semantic one, an axis not studied in their geometry-centered analysis. For a fair semantic comparison, every state is additionally mapped, post-hoc, to the same OSM label vocabulary (its dominant aspect), and evaluated against the ground-truth per-point label sequences.

\textbf{Geolife dataset}

rather than a full re-run. GeoLife is session-structured, so we keep three distinct levels: the user,
the session and points: transitions are

We selected a Beijing study area

\subsection{Operating point: choosing $m$ and $k$ under fidelity and empirical disclosure}
\label{sec:mk}

The semantic resolution $m$ and the minimum-support parameter $k$
jointly determine the generative alphabet. We therefore choose their operating point by considering both semantic fidelity and empirical record-level disclosure, rather than fidelity alone
(Figure\ref{fig:mk}, Paris-OSM, all 581 individuals, $N=200$ synthetic trajectories).

Fidelity alone would favor $m=1$, which achieves the lowest semantic generation TV (approximately 0.10). However, this coarse representation, with only about 50 states, also produces substantial record-level overlap: approximately 28\% of the generated sequences satisfy our near-copy criterion and a small number are exact copies. Because the alphabet is highly compressed, part of this overlap may arise from representation-induced collisions rather than literal memorization.
We therefore interpret this configuration as having the least favorable empirical disclosure profile among the tested settings.

At $m=2$, near-copy and exact-copy rates fall to zero in this experiment
and $\Delta$DCR remains clearly positive, while semantic generation TV
remains in the range 0.27--0.29. Within this resolution, $k=5$ provides
the best fidelity among the tested values. Increasing the semantic resolution to $m=3$ further separates generated records from the
training data, but also produces a substantially larger and more sparse
state space, with a corresponding loss in generation fidelity.

We therefore adopt $m=2,k=5$ as a balanced operating point for the subsequent controlled experiments. This analysis also illustrates an important principle of our evaluation: distributional fidelity alone is
not sufficient to characterize a synthetic release, and should be considered together with empirical record-level disclosure.

\subsection{RQ1 - The role of the semantic abstraction}
\label{sec:abstraction}

RQ1 asks whether semantics are useful not only for describing mobility, but also for generating it. We therefore keep the state-space budget and the sequence generator fixed and vary only how the state
space is constructed. As expected, MAT-Sum preserves semantic information better than purely geometric abstractions. More importantly, it also produces a transition space that the same Markov
generator can reproduce more accurately. Table \ref{tab:abstraction} and Figure \ref{fig:abstraction}
quantify these two effects. As expected, an abstraction explicitly constructed from semantic context preserves OSM-level semantics better than purely geometric alternatives. More importantly, this advantage is not limited to the semantic evaluation space: under the same state budget and the same Markov generator, MAT-Sum also yields substantially lower generation error within its own native state space. This suggests that semantic abstraction not only preserves the target information, but also organizes mobility into a transition space that is easier to model.
In Table ~\ref{tab:abstraction}, three abstractions are reduced to approximately 328 states and coupled with the same first-order Markov generator. Values are means and 95\% confidence intervals over the same 20 paired user samples used in Fig. \ref{fig:abstraction}. Metrics are grouped by measurement space: bigram TV is evaluated within each representation’s native state space, whereas distortion and generation TV are evaluated against the same OSM-label ground truth. Copies denote the mean number of generated state sequences that exactly match a sequence in the corresponding sampled real training set, per run, out of N=200 synthetic trajectories. 

Two effects emerge. First, MAT-Sum produces a state space that is easier for the same generator to reproduce. Second, it loses considerably less semantic information during abstraction.
Quantitatively we can notice  the following facts: 
(i)~It roughly halves the generic state-level bigram-TV (0.32 vs.\
0.59--0.62): semantically-defined states induce more regular, learnable transitions than arbitrary
spatial ones. (ii)~It loses far less structure to the abstraction itself - the semantic
distortion (real states mapped back to labels vs.\ ground truth) is 0.28 against 0.47--0.54,
because a spatial cell inevitably mixes distinct semantic contexts, whereas a \textsc{MAT-Sum} state is
semantically coherent by construction. (iii)~Its synthetic data reproduces the real semantic label-transition distribution markedly better (0.27 vs.\ 0.43--0.46). It also exhibits the lowest exact-copy rate among the three representations. The role of MAT-Sum is thus neither the state count nor the generator: building the state space from semantics preserves structure that spatial abstractions cannot represent at the same resolution.

As expected from its semantic construction, MAT-Sum introduces substantially less semantic distortion than purely geometric abstractions.

\paragraph{Robustness to the state budget.}
A natural objection is that \textsc{MAT-Sum} wins merely because it induces a smaller or simpler state space than raw GPS. Two aspects of the design already exclude this: all abstractions are compared at a \emph{matched} number of states under an identical generator, and fidelity is measured in a common OSM-label space shared by every method, which neutralizes any alphabet-size effect. Figure~\ref{fig:abstraction-budget} confirms the point across the whole range. Sweeping the matched budget from $40$ to $480$ states, the semantic abstraction attains the lowest semantic generation TV \emph{among the matched-budget abstractions} at every budget (e.g.\ at ${\sim}320$ matched states, $0.27$ versus $0.51$ grid and $0.43$ cluster), with an interior optimum near the $\sim\!328$-state operating point used above. Crucially, the memorization panel runs opposite to the objection: a smaller alphabet does make generation easier, but precisely \emph{by} collapsing onto real records, and the geometric baselines do exactly that as the budget shrinks (up to $76$ and $54$ exact copies out of $200$ at $40$ states), whereas \textsc{MAT-Sum} stays at $\le\!0.4$ throughout. Better fidelity together with near-zero memorization at matched size therefore cannot be attributed to fewer or simpler states, but to \emph{how} the states are formed.

\paragraph{Is any coarse semantics enough?}
To check whether the effect is specific to \textsc{MAT-Sum} or would follow from \emph{any} semantic
labelling, we add a deliberately trivial baseline, \emph{Top1-label-Markov}: each stop is replaced by
its single dominant OSM label and an order-1 Markov is fit directly in that ${\sim}59$-symbol space
(Fig.~\ref{fig:abstraction-budget}, star). We report the outcome plainly, as it is instructive. On
semantic generation TV the trivial baseline is in fact the \emph{best} of all methods (bigram $0.09$,
trigram $0.53$ at the operating point, against $0.27/0.57$ for \textsc{MAT-Sum}). This is a limitation
of fidelity as a sole criterion rather than a strength of the baseline: gen-TV is a \emph{native}
metric for Top1, which generates directly in the label space on which it is scored---an order-1 Markov
reproduces the bigram statistics of its own output space almost by construction---whereas for
\textsc{MAT-Sum} and the geometric abstractions the same metric is measured only after projecting a
richer state space down to labels. The trivial baseline therefore wins the metric by discarding
exactly the structure the metric cannot see, and its cost surfaces where fidelity cannot hide it.
First, privacy: pinned to a coarse alphabet it memorizes an order of magnitude more than
\textsc{MAT-Sum} ($2.4$ vs $0.15$ exact copies out of $200$) and cannot be refined, since single
labels admit no finer resolution, whereas \textsc{MAT-Sum} reaches $320$ states at $\approx0$ copies.
Second, representation: collapsing every location to one label discards the multi-aspect (mixed-use)
context and cannot support the geographic instantiation used at release time. Third, it exposes no
fidelity--privacy operating point to tune. The contribution of \textsc{MAT-Sum} is thus not a lower
fidelity number but a semantic state space that couples fine resolution, near-zero memorization,
multi-aspect expressiveness, and a tunable budget---none of which a trivial single-label summarization
provides.

\subsection{RQ3 - Generator capacity and privacy–fidelity trade-off}
\label{sec:rq3}

RQ3 asks whether, once a good semantic representation is available, a more complex generator is actually beneficial.
Given a fixed \textsc{MAT-Sum} representation, we test if increasing generator capacity buys better semantic fidelity, and at what disclosure cost. We compare two generators: the order-1 Markov and a small LSTM language model over the same per-sample symbol vocabulary, holding everything else identical: the same nested paired user samples as RQ2
($D_{20}\!\subset\!D_{50}\!\subset\!D_{100}\!\subset\!D_{300}\!\subset\!D_{581}$, 20 replicates),
$m{=}2,k{=}5$, the same empirical length distribution, the same dwell model, and $N{=}200$ synthetic
trajectories per run. We report three quantities per $(n,\text{model})$: the \emph{semantic
generation TV} (quality in the fixed OSM-label space); the \emph{near-copy rate}, i.e.\ the fraction
of the $200$ synthetic trajectories whose maximum MUITAS similarity to any training individual is
$\ge0.95$ (the central empirical-disclosure metric); and
$\Delta\mathrm{DCR}=\mathrm{DCR}_{\text{synth}}-\mathrm{DCR}_{\text{base}}$ against the matched
per-sample real-to-real baseline. Exact copies are recorded as a secondary measure.

Table~\ref{tab:rq3} and Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} show that additional capacity does not provide a uniform gain. The order-1 Markov achieves better first-order semantic fidelity at every $n$ (e.g.\ $0.421\pm0.015$ vs.\ $0.483\pm0.013$ at $n{=}20$, and $0.271\pm0.002$ vs.\ $0.314\pm0.006$ at $n{=}581$), whereas the LSTM consistently lowers trigram TV (paired sign-flip test, Markov$-$LSTM $=+0.018$ to $+0.049$, all $p<10^{-3}$). Thus, the LSTM does recover a higher-order sequential structure that an order-1 process cannot model explicitly, but this gain does not extend to first-order fidelity.

The higher-order gain is accompanied by a less favorable disclosure profile, especially when data are scarce. At $n{=}20$, the LSTM near-copy rate reaches $3.5\%$ against $0.1\%$ for the Markov, and its $\Delta\mathrm{DCR}$ is lower at every $n$, becoming positive only at $n{=}100$ whereas the Markov does so by $n{=}50$. The gap narrows as the training population grows, but it is sharpest in the small-data regime that motivates the work.

\emph{Summary.} The answer is not uniformly yes: the LSTM captures longer-range structure, but the Markov provides better first-order fidelity and a a more conservative empirical disclosure profile. At a fixed \textsc{MAT-Sum} representation, the two generators occupy different positions on the privacy--fidelity frontier: the LSTM captures higher-order structure better, while the order-1 Markov provides better first-order fidelity and a more conservative empirical disclosure profile. For the small confidential-data setting targeted here, we therefore retain the order-1 Markov as the conservative operating point in the remainder.

The Markov model consistently achieves lower semantic bigram TV, with the gap increasing from 0.021 at n=20 to 0.043 at n=152. By contrast, the LSTM consistently achieves lower trigram TV—approximately 0.49, compared with 0.57–0.60 for the Markov—showing that its additional capacity captures higher-order sequential dependencies that an order-1 process cannot model directly. Thus, the LSTM provides a genuine gain in higher-order fidelity, rather than a uniformly inferior reconstruction of the data.

This higher-order gain is accompanied by a substantially less favorable disclosure profile, especially when users are scarce. At n=20, the LSTM produces a 6.47\% near-copy rate, compared with 0.42\% for the Markov, and its $\Delta$DCR is negative (-0.053), whereas the Markov remains slightly positive (+0.010). The disclosure gap narrows as n increases, but the Markov retains the higher $\Delta$DCR and lower near-copy rate across the evaluated regimes. These findings indicate that higher model capacity can recover longer-range structure, but at the cost of greater proximity to individual training records in the small-data regime.

For the target application of generating shareable mobility data from small datasets, the order-1 Markov therefore represents the more conservative operating point: it sacrifices some higher-order fidelity while providing better first-order distributional fidelity and consistently lower empirical disclosure risk. The LSTM remains useful as evidence that model capacity changes the nature of the fidelity being preserved, but its advantages should be assessed jointly with the associated memorization cost rather than through fidelity alone.

Overall, the GeoLife experiments confirm the three central findings of the paper. First, semantic abstraction produces a more faithful and learnable generative representation than matched geometric alternatives. Second, the value of additional users depends on the amount of mobility information contributed by each individual, rather than on population size alone. Third, generator capacity introduces a genuine privacy–fidelity trade-off: higher-capacity models recover more higher-order structure, while the low-capacity order-1 Markov generator provides a more favourable empirical disclosure profile in the small-data regimes that motivate the work.

\subsection{What generalizes across datasets}
\label{sec:cross}

The external validation reveals both stable findings and dataset-dependent scaling effects. Across Paris-OSM and GeoLife, MAT-Sum consistently provides a more faithful and learnable generative representation than matched geometric abstractions, confirming that the advantage originates from the semantic construction of the state space rather than from a particular city or collection process. The scaling behaviour, however, differs between datasets: quality improves substantially with the number of individuals on Paris, whereas GeoLife is already informative at small n because each user contributes multiple semantically rich sessions. This indicates that the effective sample size depends on both the number of individuals and the amount of mobility information contributed per individual.

Generator capacity also produces a consistent privacy–fidelity tension. The order-1 Markov provides better first-order semantic fidelity and a more favorable empirical disclosure profile, particularly when training users are scarce. The LSTM captures higher-order sequential structure more accurately on GeoLife, but this gain is accompanied by higher near-copy rates and lower $\Delta$DCR. Taken together, the two datasets support semantic abstraction and low-capacity mechanistic generation as a conservative operating point for synthesis from small confidential mobility datasets.


\section{Privacy and utility of the released data}
\label{sec:privacy-utility}

After answering the three design questions, we step back and address RQ4: whether the resulting release is actually useful and whether the empirical privacy picture survives stronger scrutiny. The four subsections below -- downstream utility, aggregate geographic utility, worst-case disclosure, and membership inference together with unicity -- jointly constitute the release-level audit that answers RQ4.

\subsection{Utility: train-on-synthetic, test-on-real (TSTR)}
\label{sec:tstr}
To close the utility leg of the triad, we evaluate a downstream task, next-semantic-label prediction in the fixed OSM-label space. For this release-level audit, we use the MAT-Sum+order-1 Markov synthetic data from RQ2. A fixed 20\% of individuals is held out as a real test set; a predictor is fit either on the real training individuals (TRTR, upper bound) or on the
synthetic data (TSTR), and scored as top-1 accuracy on the real test set. A majority-label predictor is the lower bound. We report the utility retained,
$(\mathrm{TSTR}-\mathrm{base})/(\mathrm{TRTR}-\mathrm{base})$, for an order-1 and an order-2
predictor (Table~\ref{tab:tstr}, Figure~\ref{fig:tstr}). The synthetic data retains
$\approx\!80\%$ of the first-order predictive signal at every $n$, providing direct evidence that
it supports a real analysis task and not merely distributional matching. The second-order utility,
however, is largely lost ($\approx\!25\%$ retained): the memoryless generator cannot reproduce the
higher-order structure that a second-order predictor exploits (real second-order accuracy nearly
doubles the first-order one). This result shows that higher-order structure is relevant to downstream utility and helps interpret the trigram advantage observed for the LSTM in RQ3. We do not claim here that the LSTM's higher-order fidelity directly translates into improved TSTR utility, since this TSTR experiment evaluates the Markov-generated release rather than a Markov-LSTM utility comparison.

As an additional release-level validation, we examine whether the synthetic semantic sequences retain meaningful aggregate geographic structure, without reconstructing individual GPS trajectories.
To this end, we project the generated
sequences back onto the public map: each synthetic semantic state is grounded in the tiles that
carry it, weighted by the generator's state frequencies. At the aggregate level the synthetic
visit density reproduces the real one across the study area (Figure~\ref{fig:geo-aggregate};
Pearson $r{=}0.85$, JSD $=0.06$), recovering the dense central core and the radial road structure;
the residual isolates only a mild flattening consistent with the memoryless order-1 generator.
Broken down by place category (Figure~\ref{fig:geo-categories}), each type keeps its recognizable
footprint: bus visits trace the road network, train visits the rail corridors, natural visits
the green areas with a low per-category divergence (JSD $0.02$--$0.09$). Population and

This guarantee holds for the released artifacts (the aggregate density figure), not for the real per-cell conditional distribution $P_{\text{real}}(\text{cell}\mid\text{state})$ used to compute it. This quantity, if ever persisted or exported as a companion artifact, would carry the same fine-grained disclosure risk as raw GPS and must not accompany the synthetic release.

\subsection{Privacy analysis: membership inference and unicity}
\label{sec:privacy-attacks}
Beyond the copy/DCR measures, we complete the privacy triad with a membership-inference attack and a
unicity analysis. 


\textbf{Membership inference.} We mount the standard distance-to-closest-record attack against the released synthetic set: a candidate real record $x$ is scored by
$s(x)=\max_j \mathrm{MUITAS}(x,\tilde s_j)$ and predicted a training member when $s(x)$ is high. We
report the ROC-AUC of separating true members (the $n$ training records) from a fixed held-out set of
non-members (20\% of individuals in Paris, of users in GeoLife), and the TPR at $10\%$ FPR
(Table~\ref{tab:mia}, Figure~\ref{fig:mia}). 

The attack shows only a weak membership signal in the smallest Paris regime ($n{=}20$: AUC $0.547$, TPR@10\% $0.134$). From $n{=}50$ onward in Paris and across the evaluated GeoLife regimes, performance is near-chance or below it, indicating no persistent membership advantage as the training population grows. This pattern is consistent with the mildly negative $\Delta$DCR observed in the smallest-data regimes. Since this is a black-box distance attack, we additionally mount a Shokri-style shadow-model attack on the full Paris population at $n{=}581$: several shadow models, each trained on a random population subset, provide (DCR, sequence length) features to train an attack classifier, which is then evaluated on a held-out target split never used by any shadow. Over 10 independent replicates, the shadow-model attack achieves AUC $0.501\pm0.020$, statistically indistinguishable from the simple black-box attack evaluated on the same split ($0.505\pm0.030$; paired difference $-0.004\pm0.032$, Wilcoxon $p=0.846$). A more capable attacker that learns from repeated shadow experiments therefore gains no additional membership advantage over the simple distance threshold.

\textbf{Unicity.} Following the "unique in the crowd" methodology in the semantic
space, we draw $k$ labels from a record and call it unique-at-$k$ when a single dataset record contains
all $k$. Synthetic records are $10$--$20\times$ less identifiable than real ones (e.g.\ at $k{=}4$,
$0.08\%$ vs.\ $1.5\%$ on Paris and $0.17\%$ vs.\ $2.1\%$ on GeoLife; Figure~\ref{fig:unicity}). Notably,
even the real data has low unicity at the semantic granularity, i.e.\ the abstraction is itself less
identifying than raw GPS traces, and the synthetic release lowers it further.

\subsection{Release quality and vocabulary anonymity}
\label{sec:release-quality}
We complement the fidelity, disclosure, and utility measures above with three checks on the released
synthetic set itself, at the chosen operating point (\textsc{MAT-Sum} $m{=}2,k{=}5$, order-1 Markov),
computed on a synthetic release matched in size to the real data ($N_{\text{synth}}{=}N_{\text{real}}$;
$R{=}5$ independent generations; Table~\ref{tab:extra}, Figures~\ref{fig:extra-paris} and
\ref{fig:extra-geolife}).

\textbf{Distribution match.} Beyond the transition-level $n$-gram fidelity of RQ1--RQ3, we check whether
two population-level statistics are preserved: the distribution of sequence lengths and of dwell times,
via the Jensen--Shannon divergence (JS, $0$ = identical). Dwell time is reproduced almost exactly on both
datasets (JS $=0.000$), since it is sampled from the empirical per-state distribution; sequence length is
close but not perfect (JS $0.146$ on Paris, $0.111$ on GeoLife), with the synthetic release slightly
under-representing the longest real sequences.

\textbf{Diversity and coverage.} A generator can achieve low $n$-gram TV while collapsing onto a narrow
set of common patterns. We rule this out with three measures over semantic bigrams: coverage (the
fraction of real bigram types reproduced at least once), precision (the fraction of generated bigram
types that are real), and the ratio of distinct generated sequences to distinct real sequences. On both
datasets the synthetic release contains as many distinct sequences as the real data ($1.07$--$1.10\times$)
with zero verbatim copies (novel-sequence rate $1.000$), ruling out mode collapse. Coverage is high
($0.74$ on Paris, $0.82$ on GeoLife); precision is moderate ($0.42$ and $0.29$), meaning most generated
bigrams are novel, plausible combinations rather than memorized ones---consistent with the near-zero
exact-copy rates reported above.

\textbf{Vocabulary $k$-anonymity.} The minimum-support merge (Section~\ref{sec:semantic-abstraction})
guarantees that every released semantic location is visited at least $k{=}5$ times, but this bounds
\emph{visits}, not \emph{distinct individuals}. Re-computing support at the individual level, the median
location is shared by $20$ (Paris) or $18$ (GeoLife) distinct people, but a tail of locations is visited
by a single individual (minimum $=1$ on both datasets; $5$th percentile $4$ and $3$, respectively). The
support-based vocabulary is therefore anonymous in aggregate but not uniformly $k$-anonymous per
individual; an individual-level support criterion would be needed for a stricter, per-person guarantee.

\section{Conclusions and Future works}

In this work, we investigated how to generate synthetic semantic mobility data from a small, potentially confidential dataset.
Our central idea is to use MAT-Sum not only as a summarization method, but as the representation on which generation takes place. MAT-Sum transforms mobility into sequences over semantically characterized
locations, providing a meaningful generative state space rather than operating directly on coordinates or purely geometric regions.

Our experiments show that this choice of representation matters.
When state-space size and sequence generator are controlled, MAT-Sum introduces less semantic distortion and yields a transition space that is reproduced more accurately than matched grid and spatial-clustering
representations. The scaling experiments further show that semantic generation remains viable in small-data regimes and improves as more mobility information becomes available, while empirical record-level
memorization remains limited. Finally, increasing generator capacity does not provide a uniform advantage: the LSTM captures higher-order
sequential dependencies more accurately, whereas the order-1 Markov model provides better first-order fidelity and a more conservative empirical disclosure profile when training data are scarce.

Addressing RQ4, a dedicated release-level audit of the final configuration, exact and near-copy rates, the tail of the distance-to-closest-record
distribution against a real-to-real baseline, a membership-inference attack, and a unicity study, shows
that record-level disclosure is generally limited on both datasets: outside the smallest-data edge cases, synthetic records are not closer to the training data than the matched real-to-real baseline, the membership attack is near chance or below it, and synthetic records are an order of magnitude less uniquely identifiable than real ones. The release also retains downstream utility, evaluated through train-on-synthetic, test-on-real prediction and aggregate geographic fidelity.

Taken together, these findings support a representation-first, privacy-aware view of synthetic mobility generation. When the goal is to create a shareable surrogate of a small confidential population,
semantic abstraction can help preserve the mobility information of interest while exhibiting limited empirical record-level disclosure under the evaluated proximity, membership, and unicity measures


As future work, we plan a system-level comparison with recent geometry-centric generation pipelines. Such a comparison requires re-running the approaches under common data, preprocessing, and evaluation conditions, and jointly assessing semantic fidelity,
record-level disclosure, and aggregate spatial fidelity.
A second important direction is to strengthen the privacy analysis beyond the empirical tests considered here. In particular, stronger membership-inference attacks (e.g. likelihood-ratio attacks), a formal differential-privacy mechanism for the transition counts, and treating the min-support $k$ as an explicit privacy-fidelity knob would tighten the privacy characterisation; on utility, higher-order
generators that recover sequential structure without memorising, the geographic re-materialisation
of sequences to GPS, and a system-level comparison against deep, differentially-private, and
mechanistic baselines remain the natural next steps.
We further verify that the released synthetic set matches the real data in sequence-length and dwell-time distributions, contains as many distinct sequences as the real data with no mode collapse, and that the minimum-support vocabulary, while anonymous in aggregate, is not uniformly $k$-anonymous at the individual level,  a residual tail worth addressing explicitly in future releases.
